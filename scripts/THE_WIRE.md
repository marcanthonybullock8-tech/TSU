# THE WIRE
### TSU Film 2 — the "Incredible Hulk" release slot

---

## THE PITCH

**Platform:** THE WIRE — TSU's fictional 1:1 counterpart to Twitter/X (per `TSU_Platform_Roster.md`, Phase 2 — fast, addictive, everything happens through it before anyone can process it.)

**Who this film belongs to:** Amond Baker, solo. This is not an ensemble film with Amond in it — this is Amond's film, full stop, the way *The Incredible Hulk* belongs to Bruce Banner even with Betty Ross and Ross's army circling him. Marc-Anthony Bullock appears in a supporting, reconciliation-adjacent role (per Continuity Bible §9, this film is built to pay off Film 1's ending without requiring anyone to have seen it). Harmony Divine has real presence here — this sits inside their locked decade-long "circling" window (2004–2013), and this is the film where the audience first understands why they never quite say anything. Tyrese Avery, Esther Smilley, and Tyler Chapman appear in smaller supporting capacities.

**In-universe timeline:** **March 2005 – March 21, 2006.** THE WIRE's public launch is locked at March 21, 2006 — an homage to the real Twitter's first-ever post, and safely inside the Continuity Bible's fixed 2006 anchor for THE WIRE's launch (§3; no anchor had to move). Ages, verified against `TSU_Core_Cast.md`: at the film's open, Amond is 23 (turned Feb 10, 2005, weeks before the story starts); at the climax, he's 24 (turned Feb 10, 2006, five weeks before launch). Marc-Anthony turns 24 on Jan 5, 2006, mid-film. Harmony is 22 turning 23 (June 19, 2005). This is exactly two years after THE ROSTER's Feb 2004 launch — long enough for the wound to have scarred over into something quieter and more dangerous than open anger, which is the entire point.

## LOGLINE

Eighteen months after being frozen out of the company he helped build, Amond Baker builds a small, private tool just to say the things nobody let him say out loud. It works. Then it grows. Then it grows past him — into something that can end a person's career in a sentence, something he built to give people a voice and can no longer fully control, and the only question left is whether he can be trusted with what he's made.

## SYNOPSIS

Amond Baker hasn't spoken to Marc-Anthony Bullock in over a year. Publicly, he's "taking time off." Privately, he's in Atlanta half the time, staying with Tyrese Avery's family, doing the specific kind of nothing that looks like healing and functions like hiding. When Marisol Vega-Oduya's column runs an item — sourced, unnamed, unmistakably planted by THE ROSTER's own comms machine — implying Amond left the company because he "couldn't handle the pace," something in him goes quiet in the worst way. He can't respond through any channel that matters. So he builds one: a closed, ugly, text-only tool for a dozen people to say exactly what they're thinking, unfiltered, in real time. He calls it nothing. It isn't supposed to be anything.

It becomes something the first time he uses it to correct the record himself, publicly, in his own words, faster than any publicist could spin it — and watches the post outrun the story that provoked it. Harmony Divine, circling him the way she has for years without either of them naming it, is the one who tells him what he's built: not a rebuttal tool. A weapon with the safety off. He opens it up anyway, because for the first time since the ROSTER, something is entirely, unambiguously his.

Growth hire Bastien Oyelaran-Kweku arrives with real numbers and a real gift — and a philosophy Amond doesn't examine closely enough: that outrage is just attention with better retention. Bastien's amplification engine rewards the loudest, angriest posts with the widest reach, and it works, spectacularly, until it doesn't. A decades-old, deliberately clipped comment from legendary casting director Vivienne Okonkwo-Strand — the same woman who rejected Arianna twice in Film 1 — resurfaces, stripped of context, and THE WIRE's engine feeds it to a million users by nightfall. By the following week, Vivienne has lost a board seat and half her calls aren't being returned. Amond watches the machine he built to give himself a voice destroy someone else's without trial, appeal, or mercy — and can't turn it off, because turning it off isn't how any of this works anymore.

Senator Gwendolyn Ashworth-Okafor opens a Congressional inquiry. Bastien argues the numbers don't lie and the numbers are good. Amond has to decide, in public, under oath, whether what he built is a voice or a mob — and the answer he gives isn't a cure, because there isn't one. It's a choice to stay, to moderate, to be responsible for something he can't fully control instead of disowning it or unleashing it further. Marc-Anthony shows up to the hearing, uninvited, and sits in the row behind him. Neither of them says much. It's the first time in two years either of them has shown up for the other. It's enough to end the film on, and nowhere near enough to call it fixed.

## THE ANTAGONISTS

Per the Villains Bench rules (`TSU_Continuity_Bible.md` §5), the real cause of what THE WIRE becomes must trace back to character decisions — not an abstract force acting alone.

1. **BASTIEN OYELARAN-KWEKU** *(the Abomination analog)* — the brand strategist (`TSU_Extended_Ensemble.md`, "The Fixers of Perception") who takes the exact power Amond built for catharsis and, without ever technically lying, weaponizes it for growth. He is not cartoonish — his numbers really do work, his instincts really are brilliant, and that's what makes him terrifying: he's Amond's ambition without Amond's conscience, the same serum administered without restraint. He never becomes a supervillain; he becomes a cautionary answer to "what if the wrong person had built this instead."
2. **SENATOR GWENDOLYN ASHWORTH-OKAFOR** *(the General Ross analog)* — making her locked, canon-correct first appearance here (`TSU_Extended_Ensemble.md`: "Recurring across Phase 2 through 4"). Not a villain by her own bible entry — she's doing her actual job, asking the question that actually lands, and she's right often enough that Amond can't dismiss her the way he dismisses everyone else. She's the institutional hunt that never quite becomes a chase scene, because this is a corporate/legal drama, not an action film — but the pressure she applies is the film's real Act III engine.
3. **THE ENGINE** *(returning, in its second onscreen form)* — makes its second appearance per the Villains Bench mandate that it "must manifest differently each time." In THE ROSTER it was a quiet verification layer; here it's explicit and visible: an amplification algorithm that visibly, provably rewards rage over everything else. This is the film that first makes the audience distrust it.
4. **THE LEAK** *(Phase 2's first use, per the one-per-Phase rule)* — a batch of private WIRE messages, including some of Amond's own, gets exposed mid-Act III, adding personal stakes to the Congressional hearing and forcing Amond to answer for his platform with his own privacy already compromised.

## HOW THIS FILM BUILDS ON PHASE 1 AND OPENS PHASE 2

- **Pays off Film 1 without requiring it:** a new viewer needs zero prior knowledge — Amond's wound is fully dramatized in this film's own first act. A returning viewer gets the gut-punch of watching Marisol's ROSTER-sourced smear land on someone we watched get frozen out fairly, not dramatically, in Film 1.
- **Full-circle irony, not coincidence:** Vivienne Okonkwo-Strand, the system's own gatekeeper from Film 1, becomes the system's victim in Film 2 — the same "who decides who's real / who gets seen" argument the whole franchise is built on, now flipped to ask whether tearing the gate down is automatically justice.
- **THE ROSTER, thriving without him, in the background:** Marc-Anthony's company is now the establishment — visible on billboards, in throwaway lines, the exact kind of institution Phase 1 was built to challenge. This is Phase 2's actual thesis arriving early: yesterday's revolution is today's old guard.
- **Phase 2's own villain bench gets seeded:** this is where THE ENGINE turns visibly dangerous and THE LEAK is used for the first time — both now available, per the one-per-Phase rule, to recur properly across the rest of Phase 2 rather than being introduced cold in a later film.
- **The romantic clock keeps ticking on schedule:** Amond and Harmony's scenes here sit inside the locked "circling throughout Phase 1–3" window (`TSU_Continuity_Bible.md` §4) — nothing is resolved, nothing may be resolved, and that restraint is exactly what the timeline requires.
