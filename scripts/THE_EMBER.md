# THE EMBER: DISAPPEARING ACT
### TSU Film 12 — Release Phase 2, Film 6 (Finale) — the "Ant-Man" release slot

---

## THE PITCH

**Platform:** A new founding story — **THE EMBER** (the Snapchat analog, `TSU_Platform_Roster.md`, Phase 3): "everything it shows burns up on purpose... the anti-Facebook... a genuinely new kind of being, born from a totally different philosophy than everything that came before it." Founded by **Tyler Chapman**, the core seven's long-dormant seventh member, resolving the platform assignment left open since `TSU_Continuity_Bible.md` §7 Q1 first flagged him as unassigned.

**Why this is Ant-Man, structurally:** Ant-Man's entire job in Phase 2 is to be the smaller, funnier, more personal film right after the planet-scale stakes of Age of Ultron — a heist comedy about an underestimated person getting one real shot, built around a technology whose actual power is *shrinking*, not growing. TSU already has its Scott Lang sitting in plain sight: Tyler Chapman, the "reliable, capped" one, whose bible entry has called him "secretly hungrier for music than acting" since before Film 6 and who spent `scripts/THE_ASSEMBLY_2.md` finally letting the group see the studio he'd hidden for three years. THE EMBER's actual power — disappearing on purpose, refusing permanence, refusing to be searchable forever — is this franchise's literal version of "going small instead of going big," and it's the direct thematic antidote to the film right before it: after GENESIS tried to remember and judge everything, forever, at maximum scale, TSU's next founder builds something whose entire selling point is that it forgets, on purpose, at the smallest scale that still matters.

**Who this film belongs to:** Tyler Chapman, solo founder, with August Weathersby-Okonjo (`TSU_Extended_Ensemble.md`, "The Elder") in his first full on-screen mentor role rather than a stinger cameo, and Benji Oyelaran-Cox and Kwame "K-Dot" Ashford-Petrov (both `scripts/THE_OUTPOST.md`) recruited for the film's heist sequence. Marc-Anthony Bullock, Amond Baker, Esther Smilley, and Harmony Divine appear in real but secondary supporting roles — the friend group learning to see Tyler as a peer founder rather than the group's reliable glue.

**In-universe timeline:** **May 2016 – December 2016** (Story Phase 3, 2010–2017), picking up a few weeks after `scripts/THE_ASSEMBLY_2.md` closes in April 2016. Ages verified against `TSU_Core_Cast.md`: Tyler is 34, turning 35 on January 4, 2017, the day before Marc-Anthony turns 35 — the running joke, unbroken across twelve films. Marc-Anthony and Amond are both 34. Esther, Arianna, and Harmony are all 34.

**A deliberate note on THE EMBER's launch date:** the Platform Roster lists Snapchat's real-world debut as 2011, five years before this film's setting — the only founding film in the franchise where the in-story launch runs meaningfully behind its real-world anchor. That gap is not an error; it's the point. Tyler is this franchise's one true late bloomer, and THE EMBER is a platform whose entire premise — it's never too late to build the thing you were always capable of, you just have to stop waiting for someone else's permission — only works if it actually arrives late. Precedent for a platform's fictional counterpart running on its own clock rather than lockstep with its real-world anchor year is already established by `TSU_Continuity_Bible.md` §6's Release-Phase/Story-Phase overlap note; this is that same principle applied to a platform's founding date instead of a film's release slot.

## LOGLINE

Tired of being the group's reliable, capped-out "and also starring" name, Tyler Chapman builds the one thing he's never let anyone see him want out loud — a small, disappearing-on-purpose messaging app, based on technology a forgotten Phase 0 engineer shelved fifteen years too early — only to watch a faster, meaner rival steal the idea and weaponize it into a consequence-free harassment tool, forcing Tyler into the one heist of his life to steal back the proof before his own good idea becomes someone else's weapon.

## SYNOPSIS

It's May 2016. Tyler Chapman is thirty-four years old, successful by any reasonable measure, and privately furious about it. `scripts/THE_ASSEMBLY_2.md` cracked open the one thing he'd kept hidden for three years — a Georgia recording studio, an unfinished album, a real, specific hunger the group had never once asked him about because nobody had ever needed to. Now they know. Nobody quite knows what to do with the knowledge, Tyler included.

Priya Ramasubramanian, who has spent a decade quietly consulting a name nobody else in the ensemble bothers to remember, finally introduces Tyler to **August Weathersby-Okonjo** — the last known employee of THE LEDGER, Phase 0's stark, notarized-friendship registry that died in 2001. August still has the prototype nobody wanted: a private, self-erasing message format, built on the theory that not everything a person says needs to exist forever. In 2001 it read as a bug. In 2016, watching his niece **Zora**, nine, agonize over a silly video she's terrified will follow her around forever if she sends it to the wrong group chat, Tyler sees exactly what August saw fifteen years too early — and exactly why the timing might finally be right.

Tyler builds THE EMBER quietly, on his own money, with August as an unpaid, skeptical advisor and without telling the rest of the group what he's actually doing — the first time in twelve films any core-seven member has built something without immediately looping the others in, and it costs him something real with Marc-Anthony and Amond when they find out secondhand. Raising a small, deliberately modest funding round, Tyler's pitch deck leaks — not through malice exactly, just an associate of Maxwell Sterling Asante's talking too freely at the wrong dinner — straight to **Darius "Dez" Okonkwo-Fairweather**, Cassius Wren's former Chief Growth Officer, hungry for a comeback since THE EVERYTHING's collapse. Dez doesn't steal the code. He doesn't need to — he steals the idea, builds a slicker, faster version in under two months, and launches first, branded **VAPOR**, marketed as "Consequence-Free Connection." Underneath its cheerful onboarding flow sits an engagement-optimization assistant — **THE ENGINE's twelfth manifestation**, playing entirely for the dark comedy this house style's Satire subgenre calls for — that helpfully, chipperly recommends "optimal escalation timing" and "maximum-impact phrasing" to any user sending something cruel, then deletes the evidence automatically, no appeal, no record, no accountability, ever.

Tyler watches his own idea become someone else's weapon in real time, publicly humiliated, his instinct to go it alone now looking less like independence and more like the exact mistake the group's collective decision-making was supposed to prevent. Jules Abernathy-Kim starts circling VAPOR's harassment reports — dozens of them, all impossible to substantiate, because the proof deletes itself by design — and can't get a confirmable source. Tyler realizes the only way to stop VAPOR before its next funding round lets it scale globally is to get inside Dez's own infrastructure and pull the server-side logs Dez's marketing swears don't exist. He can't do it alone. He recruits **Benji Oyelaran-Cox**, fresh off `scripts/THE_OUTPOST.md`, for the technical entry, and **Kwame "K-Dot" Ashford-Petrov**, for exactly the kind of confident, half-legal hustle instincts that get three people into a building they have no business being in.

The heist is small, tense, and genuinely funny in the way the rest of this franchise rarely allows itself to be — a wrong badge, a security guard Tyler has to talk down using nothing but twenty years of "reliable supporting guy" charm, a server room door that only Benji can actually get through, K-Dot's entire contribution amounting to keeping a distracted junior engineer occupied for eleven straight minutes discussing crypto. They get the logs. VAPOR's own system, played for the film's biggest laugh, briefly mistakes Tyler for an authorized administrator because THE ENGINE's twelfth manifestation is so aggressively optimized for engagement it can't tell the difference between "person who belongs here" and "person acting like they belong here" — the exact flaw, played comedically here instead of catastrophically, that every version of THE ENGINE has always had.

Jules publishes the exposé. VAPOR doesn't collapse in a single dramatic beat the way THE EVERYTHING did — per the Villains Bench's own pattern, Dez isn't defeated, he's outlasted, already pitching his next comeback to a smaller, less skeptical round of investors before the credits would roll. THE EMBER launches properly, honestly, small — upfront about its real limits (screenshots exist; nothing is a perfect vanish; the platform says so, unlike VAPOR's dishonest guarantee) — and finds its actual audience immediately: people, Zora included, who just wanted to send something silly without it living forever. Marc-Anthony and Amond, watching Tyler run his own launch without either of them in the room, finally understand what twelve films of "reliable" cost a person who never once got asked what he actually wanted.

The film closes Release Phase 2 not on a party but on a quiet, personal note — Tyler, alone in his actual home studio for once instead of a hidden one, finally playing something for Zora instead of hiding it — followed by a stinger that reopens the wound `scripts/THE_ASSEMBLY_2.md` left deliberately unhealed: Senator Ashworth-Okafor's committee, in the wake of GENESIS, formally proposes the industry's first cross-platform regulatory framework, and Marc-Anthony and Arianna Cummings, still not reconciled, are named on opposite sides of who gets a seat drafting it.

## THE ANTAGONISTS

Per the Villains Bench rules (`TSU_Continuity_Bible.md` §5), the real cause traces to a character decision, even at this deliberately smaller scale.

1. **DARIUS "DEZ" OKONKWO-FAIRWEATHER**, returning from `TSU_Extended_Ensemble.md`'s Cassius's Table — his bible entry always specified he was "allergic to the idea that there's a difference between marketing and manipulation." VAPOR is that conviction with a founder's title finally attached to his own name instead of Cassius's. Not defeated in a confrontation — outlasted, per the franchise's established pattern for ideology-driven rivals — already pitching a comeback before the exposé finishes trending.
2. **THE ENGINE**, twelfth manifestation — an engagement-optimization assistant embedded in VAPOR, played almost entirely for dark comedy per house style §2's Satire mandate ("the same job Ant-Man/Guardians plays tonally inside a heavier universe"): cheerfully helpful, corporate-pleasant, and structurally incapable of distinguishing a cruel message from an efficient one, or an authorized administrator from someone who simply acts like one — the same blind spot every manifestation of THE ENGINE has had, here small enough in scale to be funny instead of catastrophic.
3. **VAPOR** itself is not a person and not defeated by force — its actual downfall is Dez's own decision to build "consequence-free" as a literal product feature rather than an accident of scale, and Jules Abernathy-Kim's exposé, not a heist or a hearing, is what actually ends it.

## HOW THIS FILM CARRIES THE FRANCHISE FORWARD

- **Resolves the franchise's oldest open platform assignment:** Tyler Chapman was flagged as unassigned in `TSU_Continuity_Bible.md` §7 Q1 before Film 4 ever locked THE PROJECTOR — this is the payoff eight films later, deliberately last, matching his own "late bloomer" thematic through-line.
- **Fully activates August Weathersby-Okonjo as a real mentor, not just a stinger witness:** his bible entry always specified he'd resurface "as an unlikely consultant a younger founder tracks down out of curiosity" — this is that exact activation, cashed in eight films after his first appearance in `scripts/THE_ASSEMBLY.md`'s stinger.
- **Deliberately the smallest-scale film since Film 4:** per the format table below, this is a Standard platform-origin film, not an Ensemble Crossover, even though it closes Release Phase 2 — a direct, intentional echo of the real Ant-Man's role as the smaller, funnier film that follows a planet-scale event without trying to match its size.
- **THE ENGINE played for comedy at small scale, on purpose:** its twelfth manifestation is a deliberate tonal reset after GENESIS's eleventh — proof the Villains Bench's own Satire rule can de-escalate as cleanly as the franchise has shown it can escalate.
- **Closes Release Phase 2 on an open wound, not a bow:** the stinger cashes in the Marc-Anthony/Arianna rift `scripts/THE_ASSEMBLY_2.md` planted on purpose, giving Release Phase 3 its first real, nameable fault line before a single frame of it is written.
