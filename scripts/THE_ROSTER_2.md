# THE ROSTER 2: FEED WAR
### TSU Film 3 — the "Iron Man 2" release slot

---

## THE PITCH

**Platform:** THE ROSTER, again — now dramatizing the Continuity Bible's locked Phase 1 Finale anchor event, **"The Feed War" (2004–2005): THE ROSTER absorbs/outlasts TOP8, TSU's first platform-vs-platform casualty event** (`TSU_Continuity_Bible.md` §3). This film covers the war's opening act — TOP8 mortally wounded, not yet finished — leaving the knockout blow for a true ensemble finale later in Phase 1's slate (per house style, a "standard platform-origin film" runtime/page count applies here, not the "Phase finale" spec; the full Avengers-style crossover event is reserved for a later film).

**Why a second ROSTER film fits the Iron Man 2 slot exactly:** Iron Man 2 is about the cost of being publicly, famously powerful — government pressure, copycat rivals, a legacy you didn't ask to inherit, and a hero quietly falling apart behind the confidence. THE ROSTER is now the most dominant platform in the industry, Marc-Anthony Bullock is famous in a way he wasn't in Film 1, and he did it by burning the one relationship built to keep him honest. That's a worse position than Tony Stark's, not a better one.

**Who this film belongs to:** Marc-Anthony Bullock, alone at the center in a way that's the whole point. Amond Baker does **not** appear onscreen — this film sits chronologically inside the same stretch where he's isolating in Atlanta at the start of `scripts/THE_WIRE.md`, and his absence is a wound this film keeps pressing on rather than one it resolves. Tyrese Avery steps into the "Rhodey" position — the friend who has to physically show up and run interference when Marc-Anthony can't. Esther Smilley steps into the "Pepper Potts" position — increasingly load-bearing on the business side, worried, still just a friend per the locked romantic timeline. Priya Ramasubramanian gets an expanded role, closing the film on a franchise-building tease.

**In-universe timeline:** **April 2004 – January 2005.** Opens two months after THE ROSTER's Feb 4, 2004 launch and closes around Marc-Anthony's 23rd birthday (Jan 5, 2005) — a deliberate bookend against Film 1, which opened on his 22nd. Ages verified against `TSU_Core_Cast.md`: Marc-Anthony 22 turning 23; Esther 21 turning 22 (Sept 20, 1982); Tyrese 22 turning 23 (July 24, 1981); Harmony 21 turning 22 (June 19, 1982); Arianna 21 turning 22 (Oct 25, 1982); Tyler 22 turning 23 (Jan 4, 1982, the day before Marc-Anthony as always). This window runs concurrent with the opening of `scripts/THE_WIRE.md` (which begins March 2005) — meaning Amond is already gone and isolating during this entire film, not yet building anything of his own.

## LOGLINE

Ten months after the launch that made him, Marc-Anthony Bullock is the most powerful twenty-two-year-old in the industry and the loneliest man in every room he walks into. A rival platform's dying leadership, a vengeful engineer with a grudge older than he is, and a money man who wants to own what he can't build all come for THE ROSTER at once — and the only person who could usually talk him off the ledge is the one person he's not allowed to call.

## SYNOPSIS

THE ROSTER is winning, badly, in the specific way that makes winning look like violence: verified members flooding in, TOP8's engagement numbers visibly bleeding out week over week, headlines calling it "the fastest death of an internet empire anyone's ever watched happen in real time." Marc-Anthony is everywhere — magazine covers, keynote stages, a documentary crew following him around that he agreed to in a moment he can no longer remember agreeing to. He has not spoken to Amond Baker in five months. He has also, quietly, started fainting.

He tells no one. Not Esther Smilley, who's been running half the company's actual operations from the inside while he does the talking-head circuit and won't say so out loud because she doesn't want to embarrass him. Not Tyrese Avery, who flies in from tour stops with increasing frequency for reasons he frames as "just checking in" and are actually closer to a welfare check. A cardiologist uses the word "arrhythmia" and the word "stress" in the same sentence Marc-Anthony has since made himself forget.

Two threats converge. TOP8's founder, Carter Westbrook, watching his company die in public, makes a desperate alliance with **Maxwell Sterling Asante** — old-money venture capital, the investor Priya Ramasubramanian always loses the room to — who wants a piece of whatever comes next badly enough to fund something reckless. What he funds is **Eldridge Kane**, a self-taught engineer with a real, specific, inherited grievance: his father, Solomon Kane, was a working entertainer in Victor Donohue's generation who claims — credibly, per the film's own ambivalence about who's actually right — that an early handshake deal with Victor got quietly rewritten out of the record on the way to becoming "The Donohue Breakthrough." Eldridge doesn't want money. He wants THE ROSTER's own verification system turned against itself, publicly, at the biggest industry event of the year — a live demonstration that Marc-Anthony's entire "who's actually real" premise is a fiction built on a much older one.

The attack lands, mid-keynote, in front of every camera in the building — a cascading breach that outs private user data onstage while Marc-Anthony is mid-sentence about trust. It's the worst night of his professional life, and it happens forty minutes after his cardiologist left him a voicemail he didn't listen to. He collapses walking offstage. Tyrese catches him before security does.

Recovering, furious, and finally telling Esther the truth about his heart in the same conversation where he finally says Amond's name out loud without flinching, Marc-Anthony makes the decision Tony Stark makes in every second act: he stops hiding what's actually wrong with him and lets the people around him actually help. Esther restructures the response. Tyrese, of all people, is the one who tracks down Eldridge Kane in person — not to fight him, but to actually hear him out, and comes back to Marc-Anthony with an uncomfortable question: *what if he's not lying about your grandfather?* The film's climax isn't a takedown. It's Marc-Anthony, publicly, going in front of the cameras that destroyed him a week earlier and confirming that Eldridge Kane's father has a real, documented claim — and settling it, on the record, rather than burying it the way it was buried the first time. THE ROSTER survives. Maxwell gets nothing for his money. TOP8, wounded past saving but not yet dead, gets one more year it didn't earn on the merits — a mercy, and a debt, that the eventual finale will collect. The film ends on Marc-Anthony's 23rd birthday: quieter than his 22nd, fewer people, the ones who matter, and an empty chair nobody mentions out loud.

## THE ANTAGONISTS

Per the Villains Bench rules (`TSU_Continuity_Bible.md` §5), all three threats trace to specific, named character decisions — no abstract force gets to be the real cause.

1. **ELDRIDGE KANE** *(the Vanko analog)* — a self-taught security engineer, technically brilliant, driven by a real and specific inherited grievance rather than generic villainy. His father, **Solomon Kane**, is a new addition to the Historical Context lineage: a working entertainer of Victor Donohue's generation who claims his own early ownership stake got erased on the way to "The Donohue Breakthrough" becoming Victor's legend alone (`TSU_Historical_Context.md` §5). The film deliberately never fully absolves Victor — it leaves the question uncomfortable, the way Howard Stark's legacy stays uncomfortable in the MCU.
2. **MAXWELL STERLING ASANTE** *(the Hammer analog)* — already locked in `TSU_Extended_Ensemble.md` ("The Other Money"): old-money adjacent, has never had to question whether he belonged in the room, and here he bankrolls Eldridge's attack purely to get a piece of whatever comes out of it. He's not punished with violence — he's punished with irrelevance, which the bible notes is worse for a man like him.
3. **CARTER WESTBROOK** *(TOP8's founder — new, minor, tragic rather than villainous)* — not a mustache-twirler. A man watching something he built die in public and making one desperate, understandable, wrong decision on the way down. His arc is a direct, deliberate contrast with Marc-Anthony's: two founders under identical pressure, one leans on his people (eventually), one has none left to lean on.

THE ENGINE makes its third appearance here, weaponized directly for the first time — Eldridge doesn't attack THE ROSTER's servers, he attacks its verification logic from inside its own premise, forcing the film's thesis (who decides who's real) to answer for itself onstage. THE LEAK is deliberately **not** reused this soon after `scripts/THE_WIRE.md` claims Phase 2's first use — this film's data exposure is scoped as a single-company breach tied to Eldridge's specific attack, not a second full activation of the Villains Bench's one-per-Phase resource (and this film sits in Phase 1 regardless).

## HOW THIS FILM CARRIES THE FRANCHISE FORWARD

- **The Feed War, opened not closed:** dramatizes the start of the locked "Feed War" anchor without fully resolving it, leaving TOP8 alive-but-dying for a proper ensemble finale later in Phase 1's slate — the same way *Iron Man 2* raises stakes *The Avengers* later pays off.
- **Amond's absence as active pressure, not a forgotten thread:** every instinct Marc-Anthony has that used to run through Amond first (caution, verification integrity, "don't cut corners") now has nobody to enforce it, and that gap is a direct cause of how badly Eldridge's attack lands.
- **The Donohue dynasty's one crack, finally pressed on:** `TSU_Historical_Context.md` frames Victor's ownership breakthrough as "rare and hard-won rather than typical" and explicitly leaves room for the era's real ugliness — this film is the first to dramatize that the "one crack in the wall" this universe needed might have a second crack behind it.
- **Tyrese and Esther, promoted from ensemble texture to load-bearing:** per `TSU_Core_Cast.md`, both were flagged as "the group's wide lens" and "sharper than her public image gives her credit for" respectively — this is the film that cashes both traits in for real plot function instead of color.
- **A direct tease toward a larger structure:** Priya Ramasubramanian's closing scene (see stinger) is this franchise's first "Avengers Initiative" gesture — acknowledging, in-world, that multiple founders are now building things worth watching at the same time.
