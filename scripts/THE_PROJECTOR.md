# THE PROJECTOR
### TSU Film 4 — Release Phase 1 — the "Thor" release slot

---

## THE PITCH

**Platform:** THE PROJECTOR — TSU's fictional 1:1 counterpart to YouTube (per `TSU_Platform_Roster.md`, Phase 2 — didn't mean to end up here, ends up defining the era anyway.)

**Why THE PROJECTOR for the Thor slot:** Thor's arc is inherited greatness, arrogance that costs him everything, banishment to an ordinary world, and a slow relearning of what actually makes someone worthy of the power he was born with. Of the remaining unassigned core seven, only one was *already* famous before any of this started, on a completely different axis than the rest of the group — `TSU_Core_Cast.md` calls Tyrese Avery "already superstar-level famous by 2004," the group's own prince, thinking about "audience and fandom and distribution at a scale the actors... aren't yet." A broadcast-distribution platform, built by a global pop star arrogant enough to believe fame alone makes him qualified to run a company, is TSU's cleanest available Thor.

**Who this film belongs to:** Tyrese Avery, solo — the only core-seven member whose Core Cast bio conspicuously has no locked "Soulmate" section, leaving his romantic arc genuinely open for this film to originate rather than having to work around a fixed future date. Jericho "Jeri" Avery remains exactly as locked (`TSU_Family_Bible.md`: loyal, "not on payroll because Tyrese felt obligated") — this film deliberately does **not** turn him into a Loki, preserving his bible entry intact. Reverend Earl Avery fills the Odin position instead, using material already on the books rather than inventing a new authority figure.

**In-universe timeline:** **April 2005 – December 2005.** THE PROJECTOR's public launch is fixed at 2005 (Phase 2 begins) per `TSU_Continuity_Bible.md` §3 — this script places the disastrous first launch attempt in July 2005 and the humbled relaunch in December 2005, both inside the locked year. Ages verified against `TSU_Core_Cast.md`: Tyrese (b. July 24, 1981) is 23 at the film's open, turning 24 on July 24, 2005 — mid-film, the same week as the disastrous launch — deliberate: his fall happens on the day he's used to being celebrated. This window sits alongside the tail end of `scripts/THE_WIRE.md` (March 2005–March 2006) and does not contradict it — Amond and Tyrese's stories simply don't cross paths onscreen this time.

## LOGLINE

Tyrese Avery has never once in his adult life heard the word "no" and meant it. When he decides fame alone qualifies him to build a platform that lets artists bypass the labels and networks that made him, he humiliates the man who actually wrote his biggest hit live on television to make his own name bigger — and loses the company, the credibility, and the family's patience in the same terrible week. What he finds on the way back up is the first person in years who has no idea who he is, and doesn't especially care to find out.

## SYNOPSIS

Tyrese Avery has spent his whole adult life being the exception to every rule that applies to normal people — flights held for him, rooms rearranged around him, a family that loves him and a public that adores him in roughly equal, uncomplicated measure. Watching Marc-Anthony and Amond build empires from nothing has needled at him in a way he won't admit out loud: he could do that too, easily, because everything else has always come easily. He announces THE PROJECTOR — a platform to let musicians post video direct to fans, no label, no gatekeeper — with the full, unearned confidence of a man who has never had to build anything brick by brick in his life.

The cracks show fast. He overpromises investors. He publicly dismisses an early creator community as "amateur hour." And at the platform's launch event, drunk on the size of the moment and the size of the crowd, he takes full, on-camera credit for "Say It Twice" — the song that made him a global star, actually co-written almost entirely by **Jamal Kester**, his longtime, never-billed collaborator, who is standing twenty feet away when Tyrese says it. Jamal doesn't shout. He doesn't need to. He simply, calmly, in front of the same cameras, tells the true story of who wrote what, with receipts. It goes everywhere within hours.

The fallout is total. Legacy media executive **Evelyn Marchetti-Hale**, sitting on THE PROJECTOR's board as its lead institutional investor, orchestrates a swift, clean removal — Tyrese is out of his own company within a week, publicly humiliated, legally boxed out by terms he never bothered to read closely because reading contracts closely was never a thing he'd ever had to do. Reverend Earl Avery, watching his son's name dragged through exactly the kind of public spectacle he warned him about for twenty years, does not bail him out, does not smooth it over with a sermon, does not do anything except tell him, once, gently and without anger, that he loves him and he is not going to fix this one. Only Jeri stays fully in his corner — not because Tyrese asks him to, but because that's who Jeri already was.

Banished from his own name for the first time in his life, Tyrese ends up — through a chain of small, humbling coincidences — doing unpaid grunt work at a small Atlanta engineering shop trying to understand, for the first time, what actually goes into building the thing he thought fame alone could build. The shop is run by **Dr. Simone Kester** — Jamal's estranged older sister, brilliant, unimpressed, and entirely uninterested in who Tyrese Avery is or was. She doesn't recognize him at first. When she does, she nearly throws him out. She doesn't, because the work he's doing is actually good, and because some part of her recognizes that her brother, for all he was right to expose, has spent his whole life uncredited too, in a smaller, quieter way that never made the news.

Tyrese spends the back half of the film earning, in order: Simone's respect, Jeri's continued loyalty (never actually in question, but finally acknowledged out loud), and — the hardest one — Jamal Kester's forgiveness, which he gets only after publicly, unglamorously, permanently fixing the credit on every song they ever made together and rebuilding THE PROJECTOR's actual product from the ground up with an authorship-and-royalty system that makes what happened to Jamal structurally impossible to repeat. Evelyn Marchetti-Hale, watching the relaunch, respects the work enough to back it. THE PROJECTOR goes public again in December 2005, smaller, humbler, and Tyrese's, this time for reasons that have nothing to do with fame. The film ends with him standing in the back of the room at the relaunch instead of the front of it — Jamal at the podium, Tyrese watching from the crowd like everyone else, which is exactly the point.

## THE ANTAGONISTS

Per the Villains Bench rules (`TSU_Continuity_Bible.md` §5), the real cause traces to character decisions.

1. **JAMAL KESTER** *(the film's moral center, not a villain)* — Tyrese's longtime, never-credited co-writer and producer. His public exposure of Tyrese's credit theft is not sabotage; it's overdue. The film is careful never to frame him as an antagonist, only as the person whose patience finally, justifiably ran out — a deliberate refusal to give this film a real Loki, because the more honest story is that Tyrese's only real enemy this time is himself.
2. **EVELYN MARCHETTI-HALE** *(the institutional consequence, per her locked bible entry)* — the legacy media executive (`TSU_Extended_Ensemble.md`) whose board-level removal of Tyrese is neither cruel nor unfair; it's exactly what her character is built to do, "right roughly half the time," and this happens to be one of the times. Her eventual willingness to back the relaunch is real respect, not redemption arc sentimentality.
3. **TYRESE'S OWN CERTAINTY** *(the actual villain, per house style's mandate that a fall must trace to a decision)* — a lifetime of never being told no, dramatized rather than stated, so the fall reads as earned rather than arbitrary.

THE ENGINE makes its fourth appearance, in a new form again: THE PROJECTOR's early discovery algorithm quietly favors Tyrese's re-recorded version of "Say It Twice" over Jamal's original uploads — the platform built to let unknown artists bypass gatekeepers replicating the exact bias it was built to destroy, without anyone deciding that on purpose. Fixing this quietly becomes part of the authorship-and-royalty system Tyrese and Simone build together in Act III.

## HOW THIS FILM CARRIES THE FRANCHISE FORWARD

- **The "who gets credit" thesis, escalated again:** Film 1 was about verifying who's real, Film 2 about who gets to be heard, Film 3 about a buried family debt, and now Film 4 about who actually gets *credited* for the work — the same throughline, sharpened each time, without repeating itself.
- **A core-seven member's arc originates cleanly, with no retcon:** Tyrese's Core Cast bio never locked a romantic partner the way the other six did — this film uses that deliberately, introducing Dr. Simone Kester as a new, ongoing presence without contradicting anything already on the books.
- **THE FRAME stays untouched, on purpose:** Instagram's TSU analog is already tagged "Captain America" in the Platform Roster — this film does not touch it, preserving that thread for whichever core-seven member eventually fills the Cap slot.
- **Evelyn Marchetti-Hale, cashed in exactly as designed:** her bible entry calls for her to be "right roughly half the time" across Phases — this is the first film to actually put that to the test on-screen.
- **Jeri's loyalty, paid off without being tested:** the tempting, easier version of this story makes him a Loki. The film's actual thesis is that not every sibling relationship needs a betrayal to be dramatically interesting — his steadiness across the whole film is the point, not a twist withheld from it.

                                                              THE PROJECTOR

                                                        Written by [WRITER]

FADE IN:

EXT. ARENA, ATLANTA — NIGHT

SUPER: "APRIL 2005"

Sixty thousand people, on their feet, screaming a chorus back
at the stage louder than the actual PA system. TYRESE AVERY,
23, stands center stage, mic raised, not even singing anymore
— just letting the crowd carry it, because they know every
word better than strangers usually know their own names.

He grins. This is not new to him. This has never once, in his
adult life, failed to happen exactly like this.

                                                              CUT TO:


INT. TOUR BUS — NIGHT

JERICHO "JERI" AVERY, late teens, counts merchandise revenue
on a legal pad. Tyrese drops onto the couch across from him,
still buzzing.

                    TYRESE
          I'm building something.

                    JERI
          (not looking up)
          You're always building something. Last
          month it was a cologne.

                    TYRESE
          This is bigger than a cologne, Jeri. A
          platform. Musicians post direct to fans,
          no label standing in the middle taking a
          cut for doing nothing. I would've killed
          for this at nineteen.

                    JERI
          (finally looking up)
          You didn't need it at nineteen. You had
          Dad's church choir connections and a
          voice from God. Not everybody gets that
          starting line.

                    TYRESE
          Exactly. So I build the thing that gives
          everybody else the starting line I got
          for free.

Jeri studies him a long moment — real affection, and real
worry underneath it.

                    JERI
          That's a good idea, man. I mean that.
          Just — do it slow. You've never built
          anything slow in your life.

                    TYRESE
          (already looking at his phone, already
          moving on)
          Slow's for people who aren't sure it's
          going to work.

                                                              CUT TO:

INT. LAUNCH EVENT — MAIN STAGE            [inciting failure, p. 34]

Packed house, press pool three-deep, JAMAL KESTER visible in
the wings, exactly where he always stands. TYRESE, mic in
hand, riding the room.

                    TYRESE
          — and you know, people ask me all the
          time, what's the one song that changed
          everything for you. And it's "Say It
          Twice." I wrote that record in about
          twenty minutes in a hotel room in Berlin
          with nothing but a hook in my head and —

He catches himself getting a bigger laugh than the story
deserves. Leans into it further.

                    TYRESE (CONT'D)
          — and honestly, that's the whole reason
          THE PROJECTOR exists. Because I know what
          it's like to make something completely
          alone and have the world tell you it's
          not worth anything until you're already
          famous enough that it doesn't matter
          anymore.

Wild applause. In the wings, Jamal Kester goes very, very
still.

                                                              CUT TO:


INT. LAUNCH EVENT — CONTINUOUS

JAMAL steps out of the wings, into camera range, unhurried,
unbothered by the size of the room in a way that reads as far
more dangerous than shouting would.

                    JAMAL
          (into a nearby mic, calm)
          I wrote "Say It Twice." All of it. In my
          apartment in East Atlanta, over four
          months, not a hotel room in Berlin in
          twenty minutes. I have the voice memos
          with timestamps. I have the publishing
          split sheet that gives me eleven percent
          of a song I wrote entirely by myself,
          because that's what I was offered and I
          was grateful to get it.

A ripple of pure, live-television horror moves through the
room. Tyrese's face does something it has never had to do in
front of a crowd before: it doesn't know what to do.

                    JAMAL (CONT'D)
          (still to the room, not even really to
          Tyrese)
          I'm not upset that he's famous. I'm
          upset that being famous apparently comes
          with the right to just... take the true
          story and replace it with a better one.

He steps back into the wings. Doesn't wait for a response.
Doesn't need one. The room is already reaching for their
phones.

                                                              CUT TO:

```
INT. AVERY FAMILY HOME — NIGHT                            [p. 49]

REVEREND EARL AVERY sits across from TYRESE at the kitchen
table. No Bible, no sermon voice. Just his father, tired.

                    EARL
          I watched that video nine times before I
          called you. I kept hoping the tenth time
          it would say something different.

                    TYRESE
          Dad —

                    EARL
          I'm not here to make this smaller than
          it is. That young man carried a piece of
          your success in silence for years so you
          wouldn't have to share the spotlight, and
          you paid him back by taking the story
          too, in front of the whole world, on
          your own birthday.

                    TYRESE
          I wasn't thinking.

                    EARL
          No. You weren't. You haven't had to think
          about anyone else's feelings costing you
          something in a very long time, Tyrese,
          and that is exactly the problem I have
          been trying to tell you about since you
          were seventeen years old.

A long silence. Earl reaches across the table — not to scold
further, just to rest his hand there a moment.

                    EARL (CONT'D)
          I love you. I am not going to fix this
          one. Whatever comes next, it has to
          actually be you doing it.

He gets up. Leaves Tyrese alone at the table. It is the first
time in the film anyone has left a room before Tyrese did.

                                                              CUT TO:

```
INT. RELAUNCH EVENT — MAIN STAGE                  [climax, p. 124]

A much smaller room than the last launch. JAMAL KESTER stands
at the podium, comfortable there in a way he's never been
allowed to be before. TYRESE stands in the back of the crowd,
beside SIMONE and JERI, watching like everyone else.

                    JAMAL
          THE PROJECTOR's new authorship system
          logs every contributor to every upload
          automatically, permanently, and it
          can't be edited out later by whoever
          happens to be holding the microphone
          when the song gets famous.

          (a small, real smile)

          I didn't build this because someone
          forced him to. I built it with him,
          because he asked me to, and because for
          the first time in eleven years, I
          actually believed he meant it.

Applause — smaller, warmer, earned. In the back, Tyrese claps
too, along with everyone else, no different than anyone else
in the room. Simone glances at him.

                    SIMONE
          (quiet, just to him)
          How does it feel back here.

                    TYRESE
          (long beat, genuinely thinking about it)
          Honestly? Better.
